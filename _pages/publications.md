### Demand for Interconnection at U.S. Colocation Data Centers (job market paper) [[PDF]](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1gs_1Bdv5989igK6Lh0uSWc8mNE41K6Xz/view?usp=drive_link){:target="_blank"}

**Abstract:** Colocation data centers are critical Internet infrastructure, serving as physical hubs where Internet Service Providers (ISPs) interconnect to exchange data. Despite the industry’s scale and rapid growth, little is known about the forces shaping the geographic distribution of these facilities. This paper studies how network effects and geographic frictions jointly shape ISP interconnection decisions and evaluates whether the observed spatial distribution of data centers can be improved. I document reduced-form evidence that network density raises connection probabilities and geographic distance deters connection for both multi-homing and single-homing providers, with distance sensitivity varying sharply by service technology. I then estimate a structural demand model in which ISP connection choices and data center popularity are jointly determined as a perfect Bayesian Nash equilibrium of a simultaneous-move game of incomplete information via a constrained maximum likelihood (MPEC) approach. Network effects are positive for both multi-homing and single-homing providers (0.06 and 0.05, respectively). Counterfactual entry raises aggregate ISP welfare primarily by expanding connection options, with network effects adding a smaller positive contribution. These gains mask distributional differences: benefits are concentrated among providers eligible to use the entrant, while the median provider experiences a small loss. Network feedback transmits entry’s effects across state boundaries, suggesting that the demand-side effects of a state-level data-center subsidy may extend beyond the subsidizing state.

### Network Effects vs. Market Power: Evidence from the Internet Service Market
with Marc Rysman

**Abstract:** When two prominent Internet Service Providers (ISPs) merge, they could either leverage the increased efficiency and attract more customers or act on the increased market power, which could drive away customers. This is the first economics paper to empirically study the effect of a merger between two Tier-1 ISPs. Using the Center of Applied Internet Data Analysis (CAIDA) data, we observe connections between ISPs, from which we could infer the underlying contracts. We examine how internet connectivity changed following the horizontal merger between Level 3 and CenturyLink. Using a difference-in-difference design, we find that the number of customers of the merged firm CenturyLink-Level 3 decreased by 26% compared to the control firms. Post-merger, the merged firm gained 4% fewer new customers and lost smaller customers than the control firms.

### The Making of a World Champion: How Much Does Individual Talent Matter in Formula One Racing?

**Abstract:** When we consider a person to be of world-championship caliber, is it purely attributable to their exceptional talent, or is it because they have the fortune of steering a championship-winning car? Most likely, success results from a combination of both factors. But the intriguing question remains: to what extent does individual talent matter? How has that changed over time since the introduction of Formula One as the car technology and safety standards have improved significantly? Who is the fastest driver judging from driving skills only? My goal here is to decompose driver fixed effects, team fixed effects, and match fixed effects in the Formula One race results under the AKM framework.
